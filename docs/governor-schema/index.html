The promise is quality, latency and cost. Rounds are how we try to keep it.

The cost term carries 15× headroom, deliberately — Ananth, 2026-09-10. Promised 16¢ / 45¢ / 81¢ against measured p50s of 1.04¢ / 3.03¢ / 5.38¢. Set at 3× that day, then 5×, then 15× within the hour — the multiplier is a dial being tuned, not a derived figure, which is exactly why it is stated as headroom over a preserved measurement rather than written in as the cost. The measured p50s do not move when the dial does. This is a stated ceiling, not a measurement, and the legend keeps the measured figure beside it so the two are never confused. The reason is admission: a cost promise set at today's p50 silently excludes every model more expensive than the ones we happen to run now — the promise would quietly become a model whitelist, enforced by a number nobody revisits. The headroom keeps the door open as the roster grows. At 15× the tail now fits: thinking's $0.38 p90 sits well under the 81¢ ceiling, so the promise binds on neither p50 nor p90 today.

The ceiling is an EXPLORATION BOUND, not a budget — Ananth's correction, and it reverses the arrow. The objective is to find where the models land, not to decide where they should. A ceiling set near today's spend does not control cost; it pre-decides the roster, because the bandit can only ever discover models cheap enough to fit a number we picked before we had the evidence. At 15× the bound is deliberately non-binding — that is the correct state during exploration, not a weakness in the promise.

So cost derives in the opposite direction from latency. Latency is a commitment: measured, stated, and the system is expected to meet it. Cost is currently a probe: the bound is set wide, the bandit explores, the attestation (§7a) records where every turn actually lands, and the promise is read off the resulting distribution once it stabilises. Measurement produces the promise; the promise does not constrain the measurement.

An earlier draft of this page said the headroom should be "brought back down when the roster settles, or the cost leg is decorative." That framing was wrong and is retained here as the correction. It treated a non-binding ceiling as a failure of control. It is not — it is the instrument working. What the bound narrows to, and when, is a reading taken from the attestation, not a multiplier anyone picks. The failure mode to actually guard against is different and worth naming: not that the bound is too wide, but that nobody ever reads the distribution it was opened to collect. A probe with no reader is the same producer-without-a-consumer shape this program has found twelve times.

Consequence to accept knowingly: until that reading is taken, the cost term does not separate the tiers — fast is bounded at 16¢, roughly 3× what thinking measurably costs — and nothing will refuse a turn on cost grounds. A caller cannot use cost to tell the tiers apart. That is the price of exploration and it is being paid on purpose.
Cost is BLENDED, and the blend is the normal case. MEASURED 30d A turn is not one model: median 8.6 LLM calls across 2.1 distinct models, and only 306 of 1,496 turns (20%) use a single model — 1,190 blend two or more, up to ten. So the promise's cost term is the sum over the turn, never a model's price. Do not attribute a turn's cost to chat_turns.model_used — that is one label on a blended turn, and grouping blended cost by it produces a per-model cost table that is arithmetically wrong. Recorded because this page did exactly that once.
Two of the three terms are honest today; one is a posture. Latency and cost are measured and stated. Quality is ordinal only — none < some < best — because accuracy has a pass-rate proxy and recall has no measure at all (§8). Until it does, the quality column ranks the tiers against each other and promises nothing absolute.
The fast tier's tool-set rule is a constraint, not a preference. A ~13s budget cannot accommodate a tool whose own p50 exceeds it. That makes tier an input to tool selection, which is the Tool Selection seat's Stage A — and the two designs meet there.

7a · THE SCOPE — emit the attestation, every turn

promise = the standing contract, three tiers, §7. attestation = what one turn emits about it. Different words on purpose — the promise is versioned and changes on a cadence of weeks; the attestation is written 1,119 times a month and is never edited.

fieldvaluewhy it must be there
promise_versionwhich promise was in forcewithout it "we improved p50" is unfalsifiable — an improvement is indistinguishable from a re-baselining
tierfast / normal / thinkingthe promise is per-tier; the attestation is meaningless without it

The gap this scope exposes immediately: the tier that promises the best quality is the one that does not measure it. §10 — the groundedness floor never runs on agentic. So on the thinking tier, delivered_accuracy has nothing to write and delivered_recall has nothing to write, and the attestation for our highest-quality, highest-cost tier carries latency and cost only. That is not a reason to drop the fields. It is the reason to emit them as nulls, so the hole is visible in every row rather than being something a reader has to already know.
Emit on every turn, including the clean ones. Not "log a breach." A row that says all three met, promise v1 is the denominator; without it a breach rate has no population and conformance cannot be stated. This is the shape the program has now found twelve times — a producer whose absence looks exactly like a legitimate default. An attestation table that only contains failures is that bug, written deliberately.
Scoring rule, carried from the finding below: delivered_accuracy is scored on the payload — never on signal, never on len(sources). Both are green by construction on MCP-backed turns.
What must NOT enter the attestation — the deletion rule. No lever. Not rounds_used, not extension_rounds, not depth, not model, not tool count. Those belong in existing telemetry and they are how we hit the promise. The moment a lever appears in the same row as a promised term, someone will eventually resolve a missed promise by tuning the lever's target — and a promise that can be edited to match what was delivered is not a contract. Levers are diagnosable by joining on turn_id; they do not need to live here. Rounds are not the SLA.

parser (2,147 calls) and phi_classify (1,867 calls) run on essentially every turn and carry no correlation_id at all. Small in dollars, but they are the counterexample to "we have visibility into every per-turn cost" — and phi_classify in particular runs at POST, inside segment 1 of the promise clock (§7c). Threading correlation_id through those two closes the cost leg.

45% of all LLM spend is development, and NOTHING in the data says so. llm_calls has no environment, tenant or origin column — the dev/production split exists only as knowledge about which module names are offline. Classifying spend by matching module-name prefixes is not a durable mechanism; it is the same mistake as inventing a taxonomy from name prefixes, which this program has already made once. A module should declare its cost class, and the attestation should read the declaration, never a regex over names. (rag_eval_adjudicate $22.04 at 0% is offline eval and correctly not in a turn — the gap is not all error.)

This was already written down. db/schema/060_turn_spans.sql's own header, a day earlier: "790 of 1,979 llm_calls rows in 24h had a NULL correlation_id … so that join returns a partial set with no signal that it is partial." Read the schema headers before measuring.

b. The bandit's cost visibility is not per-turn. model_composite_scores and model_winner_by_stage are VIEWs over cost aggregated per model and per stage. They prove cost is captured; they do not supply a turn's cost. The attestation must aggregate llm_calls.cost_usd over correlation_id itself — median 8.0 LLM calls per turn.

c. The attestation cannot be written at chat_turns insert time. Only 121 of 1,261 turns have all their LLM calls landed by the moment the turn row exists; the median last call is 27.5s after it. So the turn row is written at turn start. The attestation is a settling step, written once the turn's calls have landed — not an inline emit. Which means a null delivered_cost_c has a third meaning — not settled yet — distinct from not measured (recall) and not applicable. Emit a settle state, not three indistinguishable nulls.

Consequence — the cost leg ships, with a named residual. delivered_cost_c is emitted as a total over the turn path, carrying an explicit excludes list — today parser, phi_classify, and the unclassified $1.32 — so the residual is a stated number, not an unstated one. It does not wait on that fix. What it must never do is silently include development spend or silently omit turn spend; both are the same failure, and the excludes field is what keeps the omission visible in every row.

7c · The promise clock — POST to PUBLISH, and what we measure is narrower

Ananth, 2026-09-10: "promise starts once a user gives us the question — so basically at post — and ends once they see the answer, at publish." That boundary is wider than the number in §7, and the gap is not small.

POST /chat does not run the turn — it enqueues and returns a correlation_id to poll. So duration_ms, and therefore the whole of §7's latency promise (14.6s / 31.3s / 95.5s), measures segment 3 only. Those figures are a lower bound on the promise as defined, not the promise. Under load, segment 2 is precisely where the wait goes — a queue absorbs pressure by making people wait, and it is the one segment with no timestamp at all. The promise is currently blind in the place most likely to break it.
First instrumentation, and it is small. Stamp posted_at into the queue payload at post_chat, and published_at at _publish_completed. Two timestamps make segments 1, 2 and 3 all derivable and put the attestation on the user's clock instead of the worker's. Until posted_at exists, no attestation can honestly claim to measure the promise — it measures the part of the promise we happened to already time.
Segment 4 is the one to name and then deliberately exclude. "Until they see it" is genuinely the user's experience, but delivery lag is a client poll interval we do not observe server-side — and a promise you cannot observe is unfalsifiable, which is the one thing this whole design exists to prevent. So: promise to PUBLISH (segment 1+2+3, server-observable, attestable), and instrument segment 4 separately so the gap between "published" and "seen" is a known number rather than an assumption. If that gap turns out to be material, it becomes an argument for push over poll — but that is a finding to earn, not to assume.
Consequence for §7 — the latency figures must be restated once posted_at lands. Not corrected: restated, under a new promise_version, with the old version retained. The measurement was honest for what it measured; the boundary changed. This is exactly why the attestation carries promise_version — a widened boundary will look like a latency regression in every chart, and without the version stamp there is no way to tell a regression from a re-definition.

7b · How the attestation is presented — three surfaces, three audiences

One record, written once. Three readers who need completely different things from it. Getting this wrong in either direction is costly: a user shown the system's view learns nothing and loses trust; a system given the user's view cannot compute conformance.

Why the row and the line are not the same thing, even though they carry the same fields. Logs are sampled, rotated, and lossy; a conformance rate computed from them silently reports on whatever survived retention. The row is the population. Keep both — the line is what makes a single weird turn diagnosable at 2am without a query — but never let the line become the source.

What the USER sees — and the answer is almost always nothing

momentshowwhy

Never show the user cost. Cost is a system term. It is also, right now, an exploration bound deliberately set 15× above measured (§7) — a number that would be actively misleading to a reader who took it as a price. The user's two terms are latency (which they experience) and quality (which they are affected by). Cost is ours.
The attestation is not a user feature, and should not be built as one. Its job is to make the promise falsifiable. The user-facing pieces above are a consequence of having it — a way to be honest about a bad turn — not the reason to write it. If the presentation work starts driving the record's shape, the record will end up carrying what looks good rather than what is true.

The two failure modes to design against

1. The emit sits on the success path. A turn that timed out at 200s, errored, or was abandoned is the single most valuable attestation there is — and it is exactly the one that goes missing when the write lives at the end of the happy path. It must be written in a finally, with an outcome field (completed / errored / abandoned / unsettled). This is precisely the shape found in mcp_adapter.py today, where the success branch carries signal="no_sources" beside a populated sources list — the path that runs and the path that reports diverged.
2. Nobody reads it. The cost bound (§7) is 15× wide specifically so the distribution can be observed. If no one takes that reading, the bound is not an instrument, it is an unmonitored blank cheque — and the attestation becomes the thirteenth producer-without-a-consumer in this program. The report is therefore part of the build, not a follow-on: conformance by tier by promise version, over a window, with the delivered distribution beside the promised number. If the report is not in scope, the attestation is not worth writing.

7d · The build sequence — Ananth, 2026-09-10

"lets just integrate promise and promise end… for now. Then next step is translating promise to levers / targets. Then counting how much we have spent and what''s next." Three steps, in order. Nothing from a later step is in scope during an earlier one.

stepwhat it establishesstate
1Integrate promise-start and promise-endevery turn carries a promise from POST, and closes it at PUBLISH. The clock runs on the user''s boundary (§7c), not the worker''sNOW
2Translate the promise into levers / targetsthe governor stops reasoning against invented numbers and reasons against the promise it is carrying — which is what makes rounds, depth and tool count answerable to somethingnext
3Count what was spent, and what remainsdelivered vs promised, per term. Cost attribution, the residual, the reportafter
Step 1 is a boundary, not a measurement. It is easy — and this page has already done it once — to slide from "attach a promise" into "measure whether it was met," and end up auditing cost attribution before a single turn carries a promise at all. Step 1 succeeds when a turn can be asked "what did you promise?" and answer. Whether it kept the promise is step 3''s question. Deferred to step 3, and explicitly NOT step 1: the parser / phi_classify attribution gap, the cost-class declaration, the unclassified-module ruling, and the conformance report.

The one technical trap in step 1. t0_start is a perf_counter() — monotonic and process-local. POST runs in the API process; the turn runs in the worker. Two perf_counters from two processes cannot be subtracted. posted_at must be wall-clock UTC. Keep t0_start as-is for the existing in-turn timings; it is not being replaced, only joined by a second clock that spans processes.
Why step 2 needs step 1 and not the reverse. The governor today compares elapsed_s against soft_target_s — a budget it was handed, with no link to anything promised to anyone. Once the turn carries a promise, the same comparison becomes "am I on track for what was promised?" and the levers acquire a target. That is the whole point of doing the boundary first: levers pointed at an invented number are the state we are already in.

So (version, tier) was not a reference to a promise — it was a reference to a file someone can edit.

They also declined the promise_versions table I offered: still a join, one more thing to keep in sync. Three inline columns at ~1,100 rows a month; denormalisation is the cheaper failure. Done while the table had 0 rows and was undeployed — the only moment it cost neither a backfill nor a second migration.

3. forecast_latency_s is absent, correctly. The forecast is step 2 — it needs the planner. When it lands it is a new column and a new promise_version, not a backfill.

The distinction I had collapsed: the PROMISE and the FORECAST are two different numbers, and only one of them is a contract.

The promise is the tier''s standing commitment (§7 — thinking ~95s). Resolved at POST and frozen for the turn (§9a), because it is the denominator the attestation measures against. It never moves mid-turn; if it could, a turn that ran 40s against a 31s promise which quietly became 95s has not been measured, it has been excused.

The forecast is what we tell the user after looking at their actual question. It may differ from the promise — a simple lookup on the thinking tier should forecast well under 95s — and it is an estimate, not a commitment. Saying "about 95 seconds" to someone whose question will take 20 is a bad experience even though the promise was technically kept.

This resolves what otherwise looks like a contradiction: the user-facing number can be refined after analysis without the promise moving at all.
Consequence — the attestation gets a third number, and forecast accuracy becomes measurable. promised_latency_s · forecast_latency_s · delivered_latency_s. Two different questions fall out of that row, and they are not the same question:

Did we keep the promise? delivered vs promised — the contract.
Did we tell the user the truth? delivered vs forecast — the experience. A turn can keep the promise and still have lied to the person waiting.

Message 3 fires off the forecast, not the promise. A user told "about 20 seconds" who is still waiting at 45 needs to hear something, even though the 95s promise is nowhere near broken. The reverse also matters: a user told 95s at second 60 needs nothing said.
What is buildable when. SEQUENCING Messages 1 and 4 need nothing new. Message 2 needs a forecast, which needs the planner''s read of the query — that is step 2 (levers/targets), where the governor first reasons against the promise it is carrying. Message 3 needs the forecast to exist before it can fire against it. None of this is step 1 — step 1 is the boundary that makes all of it measurable afterwards. Recorded here so the design is not re-derived; not scope.
What still never reaches the screen. Cost — a system term, and at a 15× exploration bound it would be actively misleading read as a price. And no "SLA met" badge on a kept promise: a badge on every answer is noise that trains the reader to ignore the one time it mattered. The four messages above are the whole surface.

Why recall cannot be inferred from what we have. A turn that retrieves one relevant source and answers from it scores identically to one that retrieves all five — same groundedness pass, same latency, lower cost. On today's instruments the incomplete turn looks better. That is the whole reason it needs its own term.

9 · Who can override the promise — the register OUT OF SCOPE — designed, not being built

The promise (§7) is the default. It is never edited by any of these — an override selects a different tier, or clamps a term, for one turn or one session. Six actors, and only six. Anything not on this list is a lever move, not an override.

actortriggerdirectionscopestatus
1The user, in the conversationsays it in the message — "think harder", "quick answer", "take your time", or picks the mode in the UIwiden ↑ or narrow ↓this turnmode exists; extract_query_intent_floor reads "think" intent from the message text
2The API caller, with valuesthe calling service states its own numbers, not a tier — max_latency_s, max_cost_cents, min_qualitywiden ↑ or narrow ↓this callno caller term on the SLA object (§3)

5Tenant / admin policyan org buys a standing floor or cap for all its users — "never spend more than 2¢", "always agentic for clinical questions"widen ↑ or narrow ↓standing, all turns for that orgno per-tenant promise binding
6The platform, under back-pressurethe system cannot keep the promise — degraded upstream, credit exhaustion, queue depth, an outage in a tier's modelnarrow ↓ onlyglobal, temporarydegrades silently today; no promise-breach record
Precedence, when two fire at once. 6 > 5 > 2 > 1 > 3 > 4. The rule behind the order: a narrowing always beats a widening, and a declared value beats an observed one. The platform can always refuse to spend what it does not have; a tenant's cap binds its own users; an explicit caller value beats a user's adjective ("think harder" is not a number); and anything the system merely inferred about a user's mood loses to anything the user or caller actually said.
3 and 4 are the same instrument pointed both ways, and they are not symmetric. A re-ask is evidence the promise was not kept — the strongest available, because it is the user's own verdict, and recall failure is exactly what makes someone ask again. Silence is much weaker evidence that it was: a user who gave up looks identical to a user who was satisfied. So 4 must never fire on absence alone — it needs a positive signal (thumbs-up, a follow-on that builds on the answer, an explicit accept). Escalate on evidence; de-escalate only on proof.
6 is the one that is live and invisible. 1–5 are unbuilt but honest — they do nothing. 6 already happens: when a model is credit-exhausted or an upstream degrades, the turn silently comes back worse and the promise is broken with no record. This is the producer-without-a-consumer shape again — the breach is real, nothing reads it. First thing to build on this list is not an override at all: it is a breach record, one row per turn where the delivered latency/cost/quality missed the promise version in force. Without it, no override can ever be shown to have helped.
What is deliberately NOT an override. The governor buying an extra round, the completion critic extending, the planner picking more tools, a model swap by the bandit, per-round depth (§6a) — those are levers. They exist to hit the promise, and they must never be able to change it. If a missed promise can be resolved by editing the promise, the promise is not a contract. Rounds are not the SLA.

9a · The resolver — one module, and its output is a promise OUT OF SCOPE — depends on §9

The register (§9) is a list of who may speak. This is the module that listens, and the whole design rests on one property.

inmoduleout

Output type == input type. That is the whole trick. Because the resolver returns a promise and not a decision, nothing downstream needs to know an override happened. The governor, the planner, the extension gate, the integrator hand-off — all of them read a promise and behave identically whether it came from the tier default or from a tenant's standing cap. One code path, not two. An override is not a special case; it is a different promise.
Resolve once, at the top of the turn, then freeze it. Not per round, not per tool call. If the promise can move mid-turn there is no denominator for the breach record — a turn that ran 40s against a 31s promise that quietly became a 95s promise at round three has not been measured, it has been excused. The resolved promise is immutable for the life of the turn. Signals that arrive later (a user's follow-up, mounting frustration) apply to the next turn.
It is a pure function, and that is what makes it testable alone. No DB reads, no clock, no model call — signals in, promise out. Which means every row of §9 is a fixture: tenant caps at 2¢ AND user says "think harder" → asserts the tenant cap wins and overridden_by = tenant. The precedence table stops being prose and becomes a test suite. Same shape as the tool_manifest 2.0 build: independent and self-sufficient first, integrated second.

Step 2 must write even when nothing overrides. A null overridden_by is not an absence — it is the assertion the default was in force and here is which version. This is the same failure the program has found twelve times — a producer whose absence looks exactly like a legitimate default.
Do not score the breach record's quality leg off signal or len(sources). Verified in mcp_adapter.py:268-281: the success branch returns sources=[SourceRef(...)] and signal="no_sources" in the same envelope — the signal contradicts the payload beside it. And the SourceRef it returns is the tool's own text[:300] echoed back as its own source (document_name = f"MCP: {tool_name}"), so it is always exactly 1 on success and it is circular — the answer citing itself. Scoring on either field makes quality green by construction on every MCP-backed turn: one field records a breach that never happened, the other records grounding that was never established. Score on the payload, and count a self-referential source whose text is a prefix of the answer as zero. Found by mobius-c2; the circularity confirmed here firsthand.
